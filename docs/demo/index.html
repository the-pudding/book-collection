Depicted are 5,000 menus from the New York Public Library’s Buttolph Collection, curated from an archive of over 25,000 menus mostly ranging from 1880-1920. Some critics argued that collecting menus was a “wasted effort” and that there’s no reason for it to exist. But it does, thanks to the determination of one woman.

From 1900 to 1925, Frank Buttolph dedicated herself to collecting menus, tirelessly visiting restaurants, soliciting menus in newspaper ads, and contacting menu printers. She said, “[My] work has been the only thing I had to live for. It was my heart, my soul, my life.”

For example, pictured is an 1897 menu from the Plaza, a high-end NYC restaurant, where the canvas-back duck would run you $120 in today’s dollars. Note the volume of items, dessert options, and usage of French words. In the late 1800s, the idea of a “restaurant” was just taking shape and looked very different from present day.

In late-1800s U.S. cities, several recent dining innovations took hold: the arrival of the menu (via cheap printing), ordering specific dishes from a fixed list of options, and meals served in courses (called Russian-style service). That is, the restaurant, as we know it today, was invented.

Importantly, restaurants began as a status symbol. Up until then, the wealthy ate at home and private cooks prepared meals. Dining out among common folk was not “decent,” and neither was flaunting wealth. But the industrial revolution created a new upper-class generation who aspired to different ideals, and restaurants were one way to signal their riches. Here are menus from Delmonico’s, the finest French dining option in NYC.

Yet Buttolph’s archive was pigeonholed as the “feminine instinct for accumulation” rather than the serious work of an archivist. She insisted that she wasn’t a 20th century foodie—rather arguing that, 100 years in the future, the menus would be valuable to historians, but she admitted she wasn’t sure how.

What a prediction! Take, for example, that the archive is a window into early 19th-century elite society, particularly that of wealthy white men, and it is not difficult to find racist caricatures of Black Americans and Indigenous peoples and sexist tropes in menu designs. Women often had their own menus, called “Ladies Ordinary,” as they were not allowed to dine alone in the same room as men.

Buttolph collected more than just restaurant menus. She would request copies from private events, dining service on trains, and eventually meal service on Transatlantic and Trans-Pacific ships.

If you’re curious about the eating habits of royalty, the archive includes luncheons for President Taft with the Emperor of Japan, train service for the funeral of President McKinley, and the Coronation Dinner for the King of Norway.

The value of Buttolph’s work was eventually validated many times over. As menu collector Henry Voight writes, “[Menus are] cultural memory by providing historical evidence, not only of what people were eating, but what else they were doing and with whom they were doing it; and what they valued.”
