This is 5,000 menus from the New York Public Library’s Buttolph Collection, an archive of over 25,000 menus mostly ranging from 1880-1920. The collection shouldn’t exist, but it does, due to the determination of one woman.

From 1900-1925, Frank Buttolph was determined to archive every menu in the world. She would visit restaurants to collect menus. She took out ads in newspapers, requesting restaurant owners to send her copies. If a restaurant wouldn’t share a copy, she’d write to their menu printers. The archival effort eventually spilled into private banquets and events. And then menus from trains and ships. She said, “[My] work has been the only thing I had to live for. It was my heart, my soul, my life.”

But not everyone saw this work as important, once described as a “wasted effort.”

Menus were considered disposable ephemera, much like a restaurant receipt today. Buttolph’s archive was pigeonholed as the “feminine instinct for accumulation” rather than the serious work of an archivist. She insisted that she wasn’t a 20th century foodie—rather arguing that, 100 years in the future, the menus would be valuable to historians, but she admitted she wasn’t sure how.

To understand this dismissal, you first need to know that, in the late 1800s, the idea of a “restaurant” was just taking shape and looked very different from present day. For example, here is an 1897 menu from the famous Waldorf-Astoria Hotel in NYC. Note the volume of items, dessert options, and usage of French words (we’ll get into this).

In the US cities, restaurants emerged over the late 19th century as a status symbol for the wealthy. Ordering from a “menu,” a la carte, was just taking shape. Believe it or not, the idea of a set list of foods from which to choose was a recent invention. Menus were filled with hundreds of options; this abundance of choice was a wealth flex.

Before this, the upperclass dined in their mansions, with meals prepared by their servants and cooks. Dining among common folk was not “decent” and flaunting wealth was not socially acceptable. But so much wealth was created in the late 1800s that a new generation of millionaires (i.e., “nouveau riche”) aspired to different levels of status, and the restaurant was one way to signal their riches. And all the French sprinkled through menus was a form of gatekeeping: you had to be able to both pronounce and order items in a different language. Pictured above are menus from Delmonico’s, the highest-end French dining in NYC.

So if menu items signaled wealth status, menu designs reinforced it. The designs are a window into early 19th-century elite society, particularly that of wealthy white men, and it is not difficult to find racist caricatures of Black Americans and Indigenous peoples.

These designs extend to sexist tropes: women often had their own menus, called “Ladies Ordinary,” as they were not allowed to dine alone in the same room as men.

If you’re curious about the eating habits of royalty, the archive includes luncheons for President Taft with the Emperor of Japan, train service for the funeral of President McKinley, and the Coronation Dinner for the King of Norway.

Menu collector Henry Voight writes, “[Menus are] cultural memory by providing historical evidence, not only of what people were eating, but what else they were doing and with whom they were doing it; and what they valued.”
