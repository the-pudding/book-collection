This is 5,000 menus from the New York Public Library’s Buttolph Collection, an archive of over 25,000 menus mostly ranging from 1880-1920. The collection shouldn’t exist, but it does, due to the determination of one woman.

From 1900-1925, Frank Buttolph was determined to archive every menu in the world. She would visit restaurants to collect menus. She took out ads in newspapers, requesting restaurant owners to send her copies. If a restaurant wouldn’t share a copy, she’d write to their menu printers. The archival effort eventually spilled into private banquets and events. And then menus from trains and ships. She said, “[My] work has been the only thing I had to live for. It was my heart, my soul, my life.”

But not everyone saw this work as important, once described as a “wasted effort.” In the late 1800s, menus and restaurants weren’t the same as they are now.

Menus were considered disposable ephemera, much like a restaurant receipt today. Buttolph’s archive was pigeonholed as the “feminine instinct for accumulation” rather than the serious work of an archivist. She insisted that she wasn’t a 20th century foodie. She argued that, 100 years in the future, the menus would be valuable to historians, but she admitted she wasn’t sure how.

What a prediction! For example, here is an 1897 menu from the famous Waldorf-Astoria. Note the volume of items, dessert options, and usage of French words (we’ll get into this).

1. The Stratification of Dining In the early 1800s, public eating was often confined to taverns or boarding houses with a "common table." By 1869, the article shows a distinct class separation in dining. The author explicitly lists "gradations," proving that restaurants had evolved into specialized venues targeting specific socioeconomic classes. There was no longer a singular public dining experience; there was a sharp divide between the "palace of silver and gold" found at Delmonico's and the "dingy," grease-filled atmosphere of the third-class eating house.

The menus importantly reflect a period in history where the modern restaurant concept truly came to be. Before 1880, “going out to eat” wasn’t a thing. Wealthy people ate in their mansions, with meals prepared by their servants and cooks.

But so much wealth was created in the late 1800s that this new generation of millionaires (i.e., “nouveau riche”) aspired to different levels of status: the restaurant provided a way to flaunt, known as “conspicuous consumption.”

Restaurants were a wealth flex: menus sometimes had hundreds of offerings to signal the limitless choices for wealthy patrons. Dishes included rare, expensive items as part of building the status image of the restaurant experience.

French is sprinkled throughout the menu, merely to signal elite status.

Menu collector Henry Voight writes, “[Menus are] cultural memory by providing historical evidence, not only of what people were eating, but what else they were doing and with whom they were doing it; and what they valued.”
