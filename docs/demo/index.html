This is 5,000 menus from the New York Public Library’s Buttolph Collection, an archive of over 25,000 menus mostly ranging from 1880-1920. The collection shouldn’t exist, but it does, due to the determination of one woman.

From 1900-1925, Frank Buttolph was determined to archive every menu in the world. For 25 years, she would visit restaurants to collect menus. She took out ads in newspapers, requesting owners mail pristine copies. If a restaurant wouldn’t share a copy, she’d write to their menu printers. She then moved onto archiving menus from private banquets and events. After that, she started on menus from trains. And then Transatlantic ships. And then from cities in Europe and Asia.

Buttolph said, “[My] work has been the only thing I had to live for. It was my heart, my soul, my life.”

But not everyone saw this work as important, once described as a “wasted effort.” Back then, menus and restaurants weren’t the same as they are now.

Menus were considered disposable ephemera, much like a restaurant receipt today. Collecting menus was pigeonholed as the “feminine instinct for accumulation” rather than the serious work of an archivist.

Buttolph wasn’t even a foodie. She said the menus held historical significance, but she didn’t know exactly why. She mused that, 100 years later, the menus would hold some value.

What a prediction! Menu collector Henry Voight writes, “[Menus are] cultural memory by providing historical evidence, not only of what people were eating, but what else they were doing and with whom they were doing it; and what they valued.”

That window into history includes menus include a luncheon for President Taft with the Emperor of Japan, train service for the funeral of President McKinley, and the Coronation Dinner for King of Norway.

This includes what was cultural acceptable towards Black Americans, Native Americans, and women.

Women, in fact, often had their own menus, called “Ladies Ordinary,” as they were not allowed to dine alone in the same room as men.

The menus importantly reflect a period in history where the modern restaurant concept truly came to be. Before 1880, “going out to eat” wasn’t a thing. Wealthy people ate in their mansions, with meals prepared by their servants and cooks.

But so much wealth was created in the late 1800s that this new generation of millionaires (i.e., “nouveau riche”) aspired to different levels of status: the restaurant provided a way to flaunt, known as “conspicuous consumption.”

Restaurants were a wealth flex: menus sometimes had hundreds of offerings to signal the limitless choices for wealthy patrons. Dishes included rare, expensive items as part of building the status image of the restaurant experience.

French is sprinkled throughout the menu, merely to signal elite status.
