Depicted are 5,000 menus from the New York Public Library’s Buttolph Collection, pulled from an archive of over 25,000 menus and dated from 1880-1920. Most of the menus were painstakingly assembled by one woman.

From 1900 to 1925, Frank Buttolph dedicated herself to collecting menus, tirelessly visiting restaurants, soliciting menus in newspaper ads, and contacting menu printers. She said, “[My] work has been the only thing I had to live for. It was my heart, my soul, my life.”

For example, pictured is one such menu she personally archived: The Plaza in 1897, a high-end NYC restaurant, where the canvas-back duck runs $120 in today’s dollars. Note how different the menu looks versus today: the number of items, dessert options, and French words.

To understand this archive, you need to know that In the late 1800s, the idea of a “restaurant” was just taking shape, and to illustrate this, let’s look at menus from Delmonico’s, the finest French dining option in NYC (which still operates today!).

Tap/click an image to see the complete menu and all of its pages.

In late-1800s U.S. cities, several dining innovations took hold: the arrival of printed menus (via cheap printing), ordering specific dishes from a fixed list of options, and meals served in courses (called Russian-style service). That is, the restaurant, as we know it today, was invented. Therefore, menus from this period are early artifacts of a dining model that, today, feels timeless.

Up until then, the wealthy ate at home and private cooks prepared meals. Dining out among common folk was not “decent,” and neither was flaunting wealth.

Yet, the industrial revolution created a new upper-class generation who aspired to different ideals, and restaurants were one way to signal their riches. That is, the restaurant began as a status symbol.

Yet Buttolph’s archive was pigeonholed as the “feminine instinct for accumulation,” rather than the serious work of an archivist. She insisted that she wasn’t a 20th century foodie; she argued that, 100 years into the future, the menus would be valuable to historians, but she admitted she wasn’t sure how.

What a prediction! Take, for example, that the archive is a window into early 19th-century elite society, particularly that of wealthy white men, and it is not difficult to find racist caricatures of Black Americans and Indigenous peoples and sexist tropes in menu designs. Women often had their own menus, called “Ladies Ordinary,” as they were not allowed to dine alone in the same room as men.

If you’re curious about the eating habits of royalty, the archive includes luncheons for President Taft with the Emperor of Japan, train service for the funeral of President McKinley, and the coronation dinner for the King of Norway.

Buttolph collected more than just restaurant menus. She would request copies from private events, dining service on trains, and eventually meal service on Transatlantic and Trans-Pacific ships.

The value of Buttolph’s work was eventually validated many times over. As menu collector Henry Voight writes, “[Menus are] cultural memory by providing historical evidence, not only of what people were eating, but what else they were doing and with whom they were doing it; and what they valued.”
